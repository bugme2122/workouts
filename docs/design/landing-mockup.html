What it is

A strength-endurance circuit built around one kettlebell and whatever else is in the garage. Each block runs five intervals — 60 seconds on, then 50, 40, 30, 20 — with the rest shrinking alongside the work. Everyone rotates one station at the end of a block, so with two people you are always at opposite ends of the circuit and never waiting for the bell.

The descending ladder is the point: the work gets shorter exactly as your grip and lungs give out, so the last interval of a block is the one you can still finish honestly.

How it runs

Five seconds of lead-in, then the first work interval. The timer calls the work, the rest, and the rotation; at 5 seconds it counts you down, and it chimes once at the halfway point of anything over 12 seconds. Screen stays awake the whole time, and the session is recorded when you finish — or when you stop early, marked as stopped.

Stations

Good for

Two people sharing one bell, 30 minutes, and a floor you don't mind sweating on. Skip it if you are short on grip strength today — the swings and the clean & press stack up fast. Run it solo by dropping to one person; the app announces each station by name so you don't have to look up.

What the app records, and what surfaces it

Nothing in the app records a completed workout today — this is the piece that makes History, "Pick up where you left off", and "Your history with this one" real. Two collections, both scoped by userId like everything else:

A session is written when a run ends, finished or not: when it started and ended, where it came from (catalog workout id, saved setup, uploaded workout), a snapshot of the stations and ladder as they were run, planned seconds vs. seconds actually completed, whether it reached the end, and who was in it. The snapshot matters — editing a workout later must not rewrite what you did last month.

A set log is optional and attaches to a session: exercise, sets, reps, weight, and whether it was typed or dictated. This is what the voice logging in the plan feeds.

Three reads cover every screen here: the most recent session (landing hero), weekly totals for the chart (one aggregate request, not 31 documents), and a paged session list. A per-workout rollup answers "your history with this one".

Failure and emptiness. If the session write fails, the timer must not care — it queues locally and retries on the next sync, the same way config saves already degrade. With no history at all, the chart is replaced by one line: "No sessions yet. Finish a workout and it shows up here."

Why the bars changed color

The first pass used the brand lime for work and amber for rest. Measured against a colorblindness model, that pair separates by ΔE 0.7 under protanopia — for a red-green colorblind viewer the two marks are the same color, so the strip stops carrying information. Bar height rescues it, but color shouldn't need rescuing.

Steel #1565c0 and rust #c2410c measure ΔE 25.2 protan and 31.9 in normal vision, both above 3:1 contrast on white. Rust reads as oxidized iron and steel as the bell itself, which suits the subject better than a lime that was only ever the timer's "go" color. Brand lime keeps the app mark and the dark timer, where nothing has to be told apart from it.

The alternative in the switcher, moss + amber, also passes every check but sits at ΔE 7.9 protan — legal only because bar height and the gap between marks already encode the same thing twice. Steel & rust is the safer default.

Chart bars reuse the work color in a lighter step, with the current week at full strength. One hue, light to dark: magnitude, not identity.

What each landing section is for, and what data it needs

Pick up where you left off is the hero because it is the fastest path to the only thing that matters — the timer running. It reads the most recent session; before any session exists it falls back to the saved active config, which the app already has.

My workouts unifies four things behind one list and a filter rather than four half-empty sections: Saved setups are the named ladder presets the settings sheet already writes, Uploaded are regimen@1 JSON workouts, Edited by me are catalog workouts you changed and kept, Pinned is a new flag per workout id. Only pinning and "edited by me" need new storage.

Swap in something new is the free-API slot. The open wger exercise database needs no key — but the app's CSP is connect-src 'self', so the browser must not call it directly. Our own server fetches and caches one exercise a day at /api/discover; the CSP stays untouched and no third party sees your users.

Empty states. With nothing saved, My workouts reads: "Nothing saved yet. Customize any workout and save the setup — it shows up here on every device you sign in on."

Why the rest of it looks like this

The interval strip is the one loud thing. Every workout in this app is a rhythm, so each is drawn as its own rhythm: bar height is seconds of work, the rust rule underneath is the rest. A Tabata reads as a flat picket fence, the KB ladder as a staircase walking down, the pyramid as a hill. Real data, three sizes — in a row, on the hero, on the detail page.

Light lobby, dark gym floor. The landing page extends the welcome screen's light surface — white, #101322 ink. Anton and the panel chrome stay where they belong: on the timer. The dark strip at the bottom of the detail page shows the handoff.

No dashboard chrome. No stat tiles: the summary is a sentence, because three numbers in boxes would say the same thing louder. The only bordered boxes are the ones you act on. The nav carries counts because "History 31" is worth knowing before you click.

Copy is coach's-whiteboard, not marketing. Second person, concrete numbers, no exclamation marks. "Ready when you are" over "Welcome back!"; "Start workout" on the button and on the thing it starts. A session that ended early says stopped early, not incomplete — it is a fact, not a judgement.

What the workout page needs in catalog.js

The catalog has names and a category string today. The detail page needs four new fields per workout, all plain strings — no schema change, no share-link impact:

blurb — one line, under 70 characters, used on cards and as the lede.
about — two short paragraphs: what it trains and why it is shaped this way.
runs — how a block actually goes, including rotation.
goodFor — who it suits, and when to skip it.

Length, station count, ladder shape and gear are derived from data already there, so they never go stale. Gear is the distinct gear values across the stations.
